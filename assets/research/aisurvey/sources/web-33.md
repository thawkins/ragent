MIT CISR researchers Stephanie Woerner and colleagues argue in “AI Value Creation: Five Provocative Propositions” that AI success depends less on adoption speed than on management capabilities built around broadly available technology. They contend that trust in autonomous AI agents must be built into infrastructure through auditability, contestability, monitoring, and controls—illustrated by a financial services firm whose third-party lending agent developed subtle systemic bias after training-data drift—and that the real skills risk is losing domain expertise needed to evaluate polished but incorrect AI output. They also argue that proprietary, nonreplicable data is a key differentiator (citing Belden’s trusted industrial data), that the number of AI agents built is a poor progress measure because evaluation, governance, monitoring, and reliable scaling are harder, and that AI may disappoint short term before exceeding expectations in a decade as organizations redesign workflows, roles, and human-AI handoffs.
