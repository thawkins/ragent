Nate Herkelman's LinkedIn post demystifies "agent loops," arguing the concept is simpler than hype suggests: a loop is just an agent reasoning about what to do, acting, observing the result, and repeating until it hits a stop condition—summarized as "reason, act, observe" (or trigger, action, stop condition). He identifies verification as the critical piece, saying a loop is only as good as its "done" check, which requires defining an objective goal and how the agent verifies it (e.g., a game must be checked visually, functionally, and by playing it). Citing figures like Boris Cherny and Peter Steinberger who say they write loops instead of prompting coding agents, Herkelman pushes back on the "run fleets of agents 24/7" trend, arguing that while round-the-clock loops may 10x output for software teams, knowledge workers don't need them; he personally runs "one chunky loop" in Claude Code before bed and iterates on the output himself the next morning. He demonstrated the approach with demos including a thumbnail scorer, a spinning Three.js plane, and an Abbey Road recreation in pure code—noting loops aren't meant to one-shot perfect output but to get closer on the first try by automating feedback and iteration—and promotes a 14-minute YouTube video covering verification, three ways to build a loop, and the live demos.
