The authors identify a key limitation in instruction‑tuned large language models (LLMs): they underperform on
low‑resource languages written in non‑Latin scripts. The problem stems from fragmented tokenization and weak
cross‑lingual coupling when the model must handle languages with different scripts and limited training data. To address
this, the paper introduces LLINK (Latent Language Injection for Non‑English Knowledge), a “language-as-modality” method
that augments an existing instruction‑tuned decoder without altering its tokenizer or re‑training the full model. LLINK
works by injecting a sentence embedding — generated from a frozen multilingual encoder — into the decoder’s latent
embedding space via a small contrastive projector. This signal is then expanded into multiple soft “slots” and consumed
by a lightweight adapter layer so the decoder can effectively process the non‑English input. Empirically, LLINK yields
substantial gains: in bilingual retrieval and LLM‑judged Q&A evaluations, it achieves an 81.3% preference rate over the
base model, and 63.6% over traditional direct fine‑tuning. The authors attribute these improvements primarily to reduced
“tokenization inflation” and improved cross‑lingual alignment, even though some weaknesses remain — for instance,
numeric fidelity in generated outputs. Their results suggest that regarding low-resource languages as distinct
“modalities” is a practical, compute-efficient path toward stronger multilingual alignment in LLMs.

  ✅ The Hidden Problem With Most Language Apps (Part 2) Most language apps don’t fail because they’re “bad.” They fail
  because they train the wrong *skills*. Here’s the next big issue: Apps teach English in isolation, but you need it in
  context. What apps usually give you: ❌ Single words ❌ Perfect example sentences ❌ Artificial dialogues ❌
  Controlled environments ❌ Zero pressure, zero unpredictability But real communication requires: ✅ messy language ✅
  interruptions ✅ confusion ✅ clarification ✅ improvisation ✅ responding *before* you’re ready Apps can’t simulate
  that. Real conversations can. The second hidden problem: apps don’t build *automaticity*. Recognizing a phrase ≠ using
  it quickly. Tapping the right answer ≠ retrieving it under pressure. Repeating a clean sentence ≠ speaking your own
  ideas. Automaticity comes from: * real-time decision-making * micro-pauses, fillers, self-correction * being slightly
  uncomfortable * using language with a human * recalling phrases at speed No app can recreate that environment. How to
  fix this gap (the same system we use at nwmoon): 1. Cloze recall → build memory 2. Transcript-based input → build
  patterns 3. Short output drills → build expression 4. Live interaction → build automaticity 5. Micro-feedback → build
  accuracy This is the only sequence that reliably turns passive understanding into active speaking. This was Part 2 of

  🚫❌Stop Calling Them "Low-Resource Languages” The term "low-resource language" is conceptually flawed and
  operationally misleading. When a digital system, corpus, or AI model lacks sufficient textual data for a given
  language or dialect, this does not indicate that the language itself is "low-resource." It indicates that your
  database, pipeline, or collection strategy is incomplete, biased, or poorly aligned with reality. Languages do not
  lack resources. Databases do. Across the world, many languages and dialects are rich in: Oral traditions Spoken
  archives Community knowledge Informal, non-standardized, and non-textual data (audio, sign, gesture, mimic, visual,
  etc) Alternative writing systems and multimodal expressions If a system is text-hungry and only accepts narrowly
  standardized, Western-centric, or legacy formats, the correct conclusion is not linguistic inferiority, it is
  technological rigidity. Refusing to ingest, validate, or model data because it does not conform to dominant formats
  does not justify classifying a language as "low-resource." It merely exposes a failure to adapt tools to the realities
  of data as it exists in the field. The responsibility lies with system designers, data curators, and institutions to:
  Expand acceptable data modalities. Invest in field-aligned data acquisition Respect linguistic diversity beyond
  text-only paradigms ✍👉Distinguish clearly between language capacity and dataset coverage Precision in language
  matters. Calling a language "low-resource” shifts accountability away from infrastructure and onto communities that
  have sustained rich linguistic systems for centuries. Update the database. Update the models. Update the terminology.
  Languages are not low-resource. Outdated assumptions are. Link to [IA Mali][49] Whitepaper :

  The authors investigate the statistical structure of English text by applying large language models (LLMs) to quantify
  how predictable characters in text are when increasingly long context windows are considered. They measure conditional
  entropy—the average uncertainty in predicting the next character given prior text—and find that entropy continues to
  decrease even with context sizes up to around 10,000 characters. This suggests that long‑range dependencies exist in
  English far beyond local grammar and syntax. Independent of the models, the authors also show that there are small but
  statistically significant correlations between characters separated by large distances, indicating that natural
  language contains structure across very long spans. By examining how entropy and code length distributions evolve
  during model training, the researchers observe distinct learning dynamics at short and long context lengths, implying
  that capturing long‑ranged structure is harder and learned more gradually. Their findings place quantitative
  constraints on theoretical models of both language and LLM behavior, suggesting that any model aiming to reflect the
  true structure of English must account for dependencies extending across thousands of characters. These results bridge
  statistical physics concepts with language analysis, offering insight into both linguistic complexity and how LLMs
  internalize language patterns. [https://lnkd.in/gjHbW_cf][123]
