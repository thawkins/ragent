MIT CISR researchers, led by Stephanie Woerner, argue in “AI Value Creation: Five Provocative Propositions” that AI value depends less on adoption speed than on management capabilities built around it. They contend trust in autonomous AI agents must be engineered into infrastructure through auditable, contestable controls and monitoring—citing a financial services firm whose third-party lending agent developed subtle systemic bias after training-data drift—and that the bigger skills risk is losing deep domain expertise needed to evaluate AI output, not lack of AI training. Companies should differentiate with proprietary, hard-to-replicate data, as Belden does with trusted industrial data as networking hardware commoditizes; the strategic question shifts from whether a company can build AI to what others cannot build without its unique data or capabilities. The researchers also say counting AI agents is a poor progress metric because reliable scaling requires evaluation, monitoring, governance, and reliability, and they predict AI will disappoint short term before exceeding expectations in a decade, requiring redesigned workflows, roles, human-AI handoffs, oversight processes, regulatory updates, and equitably shared value.
