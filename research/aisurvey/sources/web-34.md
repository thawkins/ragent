```text
The post argues that AI models are a major innovation, but for adopting firms the model is a rentable input; the innovation horizon depends on what stays the same in the business model, not on the technology, using Baghai/Coley/White’s three horizons (1999) and Blank (2015, 2019). Horizon 1 leaves the business model unchanged and is complicated/plannable—e.g., Mazda’s 2022 licensing of Secondmind’s active-learning platform to automate SKYACTIV engine calibration with an expected more-than-doubling of process efficiency—but yields no competitive advantage because rivals can rent the same models and Carnot’s 1824 limit caps combustion; Horizon 2 rebuilds value-creation architecture (e.g., EV battery/software/supplier shifts; Toyota’s production system), and Horizon 3 starts from the customer’s job and is complex on all sides, requiring probing options such as autonomous mobility services or bookable road slots. Supporting examples include Ukraine’s $300–$400 FPV drones causing 60% of Russian army losses per Ukraine’s National Security and Defense Council (with Ukraine saying it produces 10 million drones a year and wants to double), Swiss Post/EOC’s 2017 Lugano lab-sample drone flights handed to Matternet in 2022, and Duranton & Turner (2011) finding new US road capacity was largely matched by additional driving; the post uses Cynefin’s complicated/complex and known/unknown-unknown distinctions, warns that core capabilities can become core rigidities (Leonard-Barton 1992), and closes with six lessons about setting horizons by business model, treating H1 as table stakes, knowing the optimization wall, diagnosing complexity, starting H3 from the job, and staffing each horizon appropriately.
```
